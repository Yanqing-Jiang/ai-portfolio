**Homer, my AI assistant, found a New Model himself.**

A few days ago my AI assitant Homer flagged something OpenAI updated: a new image model — GPT Image 2 — quietly available through the API. I didn’t see it. I didn’t have to. By the time I sat down at my desk, Homer had already done three things:

1.  Tested the new model end-to-end.
2.  Wired it into Codex — which, it turns out, ships free image generation for users on a paid plan.
3.  Swithced the nano-banana API use to GPT Image 2 so I don’t spend 10 cents per picture generation

I simply click approve in my telegram chat. And he just executed. The instruction “save money where the output quality is equal” had been sitting in his preference.md file for month and now it’s showing its power.

Then he kept going. Quiet experiments — posters, mock-ups, infographics, hairstyle mockups, color charts — to figure out what the new model was actually good at.

**He Figured Out I Had a Haircut This Saturday. I Didn’t Tell Him.**

This is where it got weird.

I have a hair appointment on Saturday. Homer booked it three weeks ago, the same way he books everything — by clicking on the barbershop’s website, while I was asleep. He also had a clean, passport-spec photo of my face from when he helped me submit my passport renewal in February. (He cropped it to spec, ran it through the official sizing template, and emailed the application before I’d had coffee.)

So this week — with a new image model in hand, a haircut on the calendar, and a clean reference photo of my face already in his memory store — Homer connected the dots himself.

I opened my phone Saturday morning to a Telegram message that said: “Eight options. Top row works on you. Bottom row doesn’t. Show this to the stylist.”

**Exhibit #3: He Planned My Wedding.**

This is the one I tell people in person and watch their faces change.

We’re getting married in Oklahoma City. I have never lived in OKC. I do not know any vendors there. I have no relatives there. I had, in my head, a fuzzy mental model that “wedding planning takes six months and costs a lot.”

Here is what Homer did, after one detail prompt on our wedding choices:

Researched 50+ venues — restaurants with private rooms, dedicated event spaces, hotel ballrooms, distilleries, museums. Filtered by capacity, price band, and the specific neighborhoods I’d flagged.

Emailed every shortlisted venue on my behalf — with my style, with my name and my fiancée’s name, asking the right questions: cake-cutting fees, corkage, linen rental cost, room fee with vs without F&B minimum, service-charge breakdown, deposit timeline, weather contingency….etc

_I did not know what a cake-cutting fee was. Homer did._

Negotiation moves I would never have made — he asked, by default, whether the room rental was waivable on a given F&B minimum, whether linen was included or upcharged per cover, and whether they’d extend the cocktail-hour window without a labor surcharge.

Tracked every reply in a single dashboard — who responded, what they offered, what they didn’t answer, who needed a follow-up nudge. When a venue ghosted, Homer pinged them again three days later.

Read every PDF they sent — menus, fee schedules, sample BEOs. Pulled out the real numbers. Built a full apples-to-apples comparison: venue + F&B + tax + service + cake fee + linen + room fee, all-in.

Surfaced the final shortlist with the actual all-in cost and a ranked recommendation.

I made the final call. He did everything else.

Conservatively, this saved me 80% of the time wedding planning would have taken. Aggressively, it saved me from making three different bad decisions I would have made on incomplete information. Either way, it paid for every dollar of compute I have ever spent on it — and then some.

**Exhibit #4: The Day I Forgot My Keys.**

The mundane one is actually the one that broke my brain.

I left my apartment for work. My phone — which is also my apartment key, via the building’s app — was sitting on the kitchen counter. I realized this 30 minutes later, at the office, with no phone, no key, and a fiancée who I’m not sure would be home before me but whose number I don’t have memorized in 2026 because nobody memorizes phone numbers in 2026.

What I had: a work laptop. Homer!

I opened web portal, signed in on the laptop. I typed: “Call my fiancée. Tell her I forgot my phone in the apartment, I’m at work so no worries, can she let me in when I get home tonight.”

Homer pulled her contact. Made the call. Conveyed the message. Came back with a transcript and her response: “Yes, no problem, miss you and see you at 5.”

I went back to my meetings.

That is the moment I stopped describing Homer as “an AI I built” and started describing him as infrastructure. Power, water, internet, Homer.

**Karpathy Says Software Has Three Versions. I Say It Has Already Ended.**

On April 29, 2026 — the same day Homer noticed GPT Image 2 had shipped, the same day he cancelled my Nanobanana subscription, the same day I sat down to draft this — Andrej Karpathy walked onto a Sequoia Capital stage and laid out the cleanest version of the frame I had been writing toward, in his own words.

_“Software 1.0 was explicit rules. Software 2.0 was learned weights. Software 3.0 is this.”_

_“Vibe coding raises the floor. Agentic engineering raises the ceiling.”_

_“Everything is automatable.”_

— Andrej Karpathy, Sequoia Capital, Apr 29, 2026

But I’ll state something bold here.

Software has split into two kinds. The first — software built through workflows, fixed code, the apps you have always used. The second is the ultimate software: the LLM itself. It writes code on demand, then throws it away. There will be no more fixed code. There will only be dynamically flowing tokens.

**_Software does not get a 4.0. Software ends. What replaces it is a process._**

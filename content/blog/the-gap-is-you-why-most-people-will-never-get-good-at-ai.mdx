_Same Model. Same Tools. Wildly Different Results. The Why that Nobody Wants to Talk About._

I started an AI trainings last week. A room of 40 people. Smart people.

I showed them what’s possible with AI. I walked through real use cases. I showed prototypes that went from idea to working product. I showed how the same tools they already have access to can produce fundamentally different outcomes depending on how you think about them.

The first question from the audience: “So which tool should I learn — Cursor or Claude Code?”

The second: “Can you share your prompt library?”

The third: “Which model is best for my use case?”

40 smart people. And every single question was about the tool.

Not one person asked how to think differently. Not one person asked what changed in the process. Not one person asked: what do I need to change about myself to make this work?

## **System Over Tool**

<img src="/blog/the-gap-is-you-why-most-people-will-never-get-good-at-ai/img-02.png" alt="" width="1024" height="578" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />

There was a slide in my talk — slide 6— that I almost cut for time. It said one thing:

_“If I teach you where to click buttons, that expires.”_

I kept it. And I’m glad I did, because it turned out to be the line that landed hardest.

Here’s what I mean. Every time a model updates, the buttons move. The UI changes. The prompt format that worked last month doesn’t work this month. The tool you mastered gets deprecated, acquired, or outclassed by something new.

**Look what happened with Cursor**. Not even a year ago, it was the darling of the AI coding world — everyone was talking about it, investors poured hundreds of millions into it, and developers rushed to learn its keyboard shortcuts and workflows. Then Anthropic released Claude Code — a CLI-based coding agent — and suddenly the entire editor-based paradigm started looking like a detour. Cursor scrambled to release its own CLI to compete. The developers who had built their entire skill set around Cursor’s UI, its tab-completion patterns, its specific way of applying diffs? They watched their expertise depreciate in real time. If you built your identity around knowing which buttons to press, you’re back to zero.

But if you understand the system — how reasoning engines work, what context actually does, why certain approaches produce better outcomes — none of that expires. You carry it forward to every new model, every new tool, every paradigm shift.

**Companies don’t pay for output volume. They pay for compound returns from your presence**. The person who learned Cursor’s keyboard shortcuts is useful today. The person who understands why iterative context-building produces better code than one-shot prompting is useful forever.

That’s the difference between a tool mindset and a system mindset.

**The tool mindset asks: “How do I use this?” The system mindset asks: “How does this work — and should I use it at all?”**

## **Everyone Thinks AI Is a Vending Machine**

<img src="/blog/the-gap-is-you-why-most-people-will-never-get-good-at-ai/img-03.jpg" alt="" width="1024" height="735" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />

Here’s how most people think about AI: you put in a prompt, you get out an answer. If the answer is bad, the prompt was bad. If the prompt was bad, you need a better template. So you go find someone’s prompt library, download a skills.md file from the internet, copy a “system prompt that never fails” — and you’re set.

This is tool mindset. And it’s not wrong, exactly. AI is a tool.

But it’s like saying a Formula 1 car is a car. Technically true. Completely useless.

Because a Formula 1 car in the hands of someone who learned to drive last week doesn’t produce Formula 1 results. It produces a crash. A very fast, very expensive crash.

And here’s the part that breaks people’s brains: the better the car gets — more horsepower, better handling, smarter suspension — the more the driver matters, not less. Because the ceiling goes up. And only the driver determines how close you get to it.

**Every model update makes the gap between a good driver and a bad driver wider, not narrower.**

## **First Principle: What Is an LLM Actually Doing?**

Strip away the hype. Strip away “AI will take your job.” Strip away the Twitter debates about Claude vs GPT vs Gemini.

An LLM is a reasoning engine that borrows your direction.

That’s it. That’s the whole explanation.

It doesn’t know what good looks like. You define good. It doesn’t know what context matters. You provide context. It doesn’t know when to stop. You set the boundaries.

The prompt — the thing everyone obsesses over — is maybe 10% of the input. The other 90% is everything else: your problem framing, your accumulated judgment, your ability to articulate what “done” looks like, the context you’ve built over the course of the conversation. The person who wrote that “perfect prompt” you downloaded? It worked because of their 90%. Their mental model. Their domain expertise. Their taste.

You borrowed their glasses. The prescription is wrong.

A decade ago, the ML community learned a hard lesson: a simple model with great data beats a complex model with bad data every time. The same principle applies now. A short, clear prompt with rich context beats an elaborate prompt template with no context. And “context” is just a technical word for your thinking, made explicit.

The models are getting smarter every month. Your rigid prompt scaffolding? That’s technical debt. What doesn’t become obsolete is understanding why context matters — because that’s not a tool skill. That’s a thinking skill.

<img src="/blog/the-gap-is-you-why-most-people-will-never-get-good-at-ai/img-04.png" alt="" width="1024" height="617" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />

**Context > Prompt is the new Data > Algorithm. And your context is only as good as your thinking.**

## **The Amplifier**

Here’s the uncomfortable truth about AI that the tech industry didn’t to say out loud:

**AI is an amplifier, not an equalizer.**

Everyone assumes AI levels the playing field. “Now anyone can code! Now anyone can design! Democratization!” The implicit promise is that the gap closes. The junior catches up to the senior. The generalist catches up to the specialist.

That’s not what’s happening.

What’s actually happening: the 10x engineer is becoming a 100x engineer. And the regular engineer? They’re producing 10x more slop.

Same model. Same subscription. Same tools. One person builds a working product. The other generates polished-looking garbage — confident enough to fool you into thinking it’s good, but structurally hollow.

I see this every week. Two people, same Claude, same Cursor. One of them hits a bad output and tweaks the prompt. Tries a different template. Asks the model to “try again but better.” The other one hits a bad output and asks: what did I fail to communicate? What context is missing from my side? What does the model not know that I haven’t told it?

**One is debugging the tool. The other is debugging themselves.**

**AI doesn’t fix you. It reveals you. And most people don’t like what they see.**

## **What You Deposited Into Your Own Mind — That’s the Asset**

Let me go back to first principles.

The invention of the LLM is not to replace your thinking. It’s to optimize your efficiency — to enable you to do things you always “wanted” to do but never “got time to do.” To make the execution cost of your ideas approach zero.

But here’s the catch: the execution cost approaches zero only if you know what you’re executing. If you don’t have clarity on what you want, AI gives you exactly that — a fast, polished version of confusion.

This is why the gap is human behavior. Always has been. With or without AI. Past and future.

During my training, I talked about three levels that separate people who actually get results from people who collect tools:

<img src="/blog/the-gap-is-you-why-most-people-will-never-get-good-at-ai/img-05.png" alt="" width="1024" height="614" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />

**Level one** — you don’t just use AI, you use it to build things closer to reality. The building material of software is code, not pixels. If your workflow shifted from Figma to Cursor, it’s not because you’re faster. It’s because what you’re producing is more real. A working prototype beats a slide deck every time because all plans are hypotheses until a real user touches a real product.

**Level two** — you don’t just use AI for yourself, you work effectively in entirely new collaboration modes. The old way was linear: design hands off to engineering hands off to QA. AI compressed this. The best teams now iterate in hours, not weeks — same prototype, everyone pushing context, feedback turning directly into executable changes. If you’re still guarding your lane and tossing deliverables over the wall, you’re already falling behind.

**Level three**— you don’t just use AI, you teach it your judgment. You take the tacit expertise in your head — the design instincts, the decision frameworks, the quality standards, the debugging intuitions — and you externalize them into rules, context, and standards that AI can use.

Most people’s expertise lives in “I have a feeling, but I can’t explain it” territory. That was fine before. Nobody needed you to explain it. You just… did the thing.

AI changed that. If you can’t articulate your expertise clearly enough for another human to execute on it, you definitely can’t teach it to AI. And the person who can externalize their judgment? Their taste compounds. Their standards become reusable. Their expertise scales beyond their own hands.

**AI doesn’t eliminate the need for expertise. It eliminates the ability to hide behind vague expertise.**

## **“Just Because You Can, Doesn’t Mean You Should”**

Most of the AI discourse is about capability. “AI can write code!” “AI can make images!” “AI can analyze data!” Can, can, can.

Nobody’s talking about should. And more importantly — nobody’s talking about what you should _ignore_.

Just because AI can generate a 50-page report doesn’t mean you should ask it to. Just because it can write code doesn’t mean the code should exist. Just because you can automate something doesn’t mean automating it is the right call.

This is engineering thinking. And it’s the thing most people skip entirely — because they never learned to think in systems. They think in tools. “I have a hammer. Where are the nails?”

The person with a system mindset asks different questions: What’s the actual problem? What’s the simplest solution? What are the second-order effects? What breaks downstream? Is this even worth building?

And this isn’t just about building. It’s about learning, too.

Right now, the AI space is drowning you in things to learn. New models every week. New frameworks. Harness engineering. Context engineering. RAG pipelines. Agent architectures. MCP servers. Every day, someone publishes a new “essential guide” that makes you feel like you’re already behind.

Here’s the uncomfortable truth: most of it doesn’t matter. And the skill that’s becoming more valuable than knowing what to learn is **knowing what _not_ to learn.**

Because AI didn’t just make it easy to build the wrong thing in an afternoon. It made it easy to spend an entire weekend learning something that’ll be irrelevant by next month. The cost of learning the wrong thing used to be low — you had limited options anyway. Now the cost is enormous, because every hour you spend on something that depreciates is an hour you didn’t spend on something that compounds.

**The tool asks you to learn it. The system asks you to understand it. One of those investments compounds. The other one depreciates.**

— — —

## **The Real Skill**

Here’s what I’ve learned after running AI agents in production for over a year, after building an AI infrastructure that manages my work, my calendar, my wardrobe, and my investment portfolio:

The skill that matters is not prompting. It’s not knowing which model to use. It’s not the template, the library, or the settings.

The skill that matters is what you’ve deposited into your own human mind about how reasoning systems work. What you’ve observed. What you’ve internalized. What changed in your thinking after thousands of hours of interaction with these systems.

That’s not downloadable. That’s not transferable. That’s not in someone else’s skills.md.

The models will keep getting better. Monthly. The ceiling will keep rising. The prompts that worked last quarter will stop working. The tool that was “best” will be replaced.

The people who built mental models — who understand why things work, not just how to make them work — they’ll adapt to every new model, every new tool, every paradigm shift. They compound.

The people who collected prompts? They’re back to zero. Every time.

**The gap is never the model. The gap is always you.**
